Mathematics

Why is there no largest prime number? The primes begin 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, and they keep appearing as the numbers grow. It is natural to wonder whether the list eventually stops. Euclid answered the question more than two thousand years ago with an argument that fits in a short paragraph, and the answer is no.

6 min read

Key takeaways

  • A prime is an integer greater than one whose only positive divisors are one and itself.
  • Euclid's proof assumes a finite list of all primes and constructs a number that cannot be divided by any of them.
  • The constructed number must have a prime factor, and that factor cannot be on the list, which contradicts the assumption.
  • The proof shows existence, not a practical recipe for finding the next prime.

Primes and the Search for a Last One

A prime number is an integer greater than one that has no positive divisors other than one and itself. The first primes are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, and 23. Composite numbers such as 4, 6, 9, and 12 can be factored into smaller integers, and the fundamental theorem of arithmetic says that every integer greater than one factors into primes in essentially one way.

As the numbers grow, primes become less frequent but never disappear. There are 25 primes below 100, 168 below 1,000, and about 78,498 below one million. The density decreases roughly as one over the natural logarithm of the upper bound, a result known as the prime number theorem. A decreasing density does not imply a last prime, however.

The question is whether the list is finite or infinite. A finite list would have a largest element. Proving that no such element exists requires an argument about all primes at once, not just a search through larger and larger numbers.

Two different questions

Finding a large prime is a computational task. Proving that the primes never end is a logical task. Euclid's proof answers the second without producing a usable recipe for the first.

Euclid’s Argument

Suppose, for the sake of contradiction, that there are only finitely many primes. List them all: p1, p2, p3, up to pn. The list is assumed complete, so every prime appears exactly once. Now form a new number N by multiplying all the primes on the list and adding one.

\[N = p_1 p_2 p_3 \cdots p_n + 1\]
N
the number constructed from the finite list (integer)
p_i
the i-th prime in the assumed complete list (integer)
n
the number of primes in the assumed list (integer)

Now divide N by any prime p_i on the list. The product p1 p2 ... pn is divisible by p_i, so it leaves remainder zero. Adding one leaves remainder one. Therefore N is not divisible by any prime on the list.

Every integer greater than one has at least one prime factor. So N must have a prime factor q. But q cannot be any of the primes on the list, because none of them divides N. That contradicts the assumption that the list contained every prime. The only escape is that no finite list can contain every prime.

The contradiction is the point

The argument does not require N to be prime. It only requires N to have some prime factor, and that factor must lie outside the assumed complete list.

An Example Where N Is Composite

A common misunderstanding is that N itself must be prime. It often is, but not always. The proof only needs a prime factor of N, and that factor is guaranteed to be new. A small example shows the difference clearly.

Start with the list 3 and 5

  1. Assume the only primes are 3 and 5.
  2. Multiply them and add one. The product is 15, so N is 16.
  3. The number 16 is composite, equal to 2 raised to the fourth power.
  4. Its prime factor is 2, which is not on the list.
  5. The list was incomplete, contradicting the assumption.

The constructed number was not prime, but it still forced a new prime to exist. That is exactly what the proof needs.

If you start with the list 2, 3, 5, 7, the product is 210 and N is 211, which happens to be prime. Starting with 3 and 5 gives a composite N. Both cases support the same conclusion, which is why the proof is robust.

The size of N grows extremely fast. For the first several primes, the product plus one quickly becomes far larger than any number a person would factor by hand. That is one reason the proof is not a practical prime-generating algorithm.

Other Proofs and What They Show

Euclid's proof is not the only one. Another argument uses the factorial. For any integer n greater than one, the number n factorial plus one has a prime factor larger than n. Since n can be chosen arbitrarily large, primes of arbitrarily large size exist. This is again a proof of infinitude, not a construction of the next prime.

A third approach uses the divergence of the harmonic series over primes. Euler showed that the sum of the reciprocals of the primes diverges, which implies that there are infinitely many primes. The argument is more advanced but connects number theory to analysis and has influenced the study of prime distribution.

These proofs differ in technique but agree on the conclusion. They also illustrate a feature of mathematics: a statement about an infinite set can be proved without examining every element. The structure of the integers forces the result.

The infinitude of primes does not mean that primes are easy to find. The largest known primes are enormous, discovered with specialized algorithms and significant computation. The gap between existence and computation is a recurring theme in number theory.

Why the Result Matters

The infinitude of primes underpins modern cryptography. Public-key systems such as RSA rely on the difficulty of factoring large numbers that are products of two primes. The security argument assumes that suitable primes exist in abundance, which Euclid's theorem guarantees.

The result also shapes how mathematicians think about infinity. The primes are a sparse subset of the integers, yet they are infinite. Density and cardinality are different ideas, and the primes are a clean example of the distinction.

There are still open questions about primes. Twin primes, pairs such as 11 and 13 or 41 and 43, are conjectured to be infinite in number, but that remains unproved. The infinitude of primes is settled; the finer structure of how they are distributed is not.

So the next time a prime appears at the end of a long search, it is not the last one. Euclid's argument guarantees another beyond it, and another beyond that. The list never closes, even though no one can write it all down.

Common misconceptions

Euclid's proof shows that the number formed by multiplying all primes and adding one is always prime.

The constructed number is not always prime. The proof only shows that it has a prime factor not on the assumed list. For example, starting with 3 and 5 gives 16, which is composite, but its factor 2 is still a new prime.

Because there are infinitely many primes, we can generate them one by one forever.

Infinitude is an existence statement. It does not provide an efficient formula for the next prime. Finding large primes requires computation and specialized tests, and the gap between existence and practical generation is significant.

The primes must eventually stop because they become rarer.

Rarity and finiteness are different. The density of primes decreases, but the prime number theorem describes that decrease without implying a last prime. Euclid's proof shows that beyond any finite list there is always another prime.

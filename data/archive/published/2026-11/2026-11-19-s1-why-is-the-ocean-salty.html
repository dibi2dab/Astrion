Why is the ocean salty? Rain that falls on land is nearly fresh, and rivers that feed the sea are usually drinkable, yet the ocean holds enough salt to make roughly 35 grams of dissolved solids in every kilogram of seawater. The answer is not that rivers pour salt into a stagnant basin. It is a balance between slow input, long residence times, and processes that remove ions from the water.

6 min read

Key takeaways

  • Seawater is about 3.5 percent dissolved solids by mass, dominated by sodium and chloride ions.
  • Most dissolved ions originate from the chemical weathering of continental rock, delivered by rivers over long timescales.
  • Hydrothermal circulation at mid-ocean ridges both adds and removes ions, helping regulate seawater chemistry.
  • The ocean is not getting saltier without limit because removal processes balance the slow input over geological time.

A Measured Mixture

Salinity is the mass of dissolved salts in a mass of seawater, usually reported in grams per kilogram or practical salinity units. Average open-ocean salinity is about 35, meaning 35 grams of dissolved solids per kilogram of seawater. That is roughly one teaspoon of salt in a cup of water, enough to taste clearly and not enough to make the sea a brine.

The dissolved material is not ordinary table salt alone. Sodium and chloride dominate, but seawater also contains sulfate, magnesium, calcium, potassium, and many trace elements. The relative proportions of the major ions are remarkably stable across the open ocean, even though the total salinity varies from place to place.

The stability of the mix is a clue. If each river had a unique fingerprint that persisted, seawater composition would vary much more from region to region. Instead, mixing and chemical reactions smooth the differences. The ocean behaves like a well-stirred reservoir with a residence time of thousands of years for many ions.

Where the Ions Come From

The main source is chemical weathering on land. Rainwater is slightly acidic because it dissolves carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, forming carbonic acid. That weak acid attacks minerals in rock and releases ions into soil water and streams. Rivers carry the dissolved load to the sea, even though the water itself is not salty to taste.

Weathering rates depend on climate, rock type, topography, and biology. Warm, wet regions with abundant vegetation tend to weather faster. Limestone and evaporite deposits dissolve readily, while granite resists attack. The composition of the continental crust therefore leaves a signature on the chemistry of the ocean.

A second source is hydrothermal circulation at mid-ocean ridges. Seawater seeps into hot, fractured rock, reacts with it, and returns through vents. The reactions can remove some ions and add others. Hydrothermal fluids are not a simple salt source; they are a chemical exchange system that reshapes seawater as it circulates.

Rivers are not salty, but they carry salt

The concentration in river water is low, so the input per liter is small. The ocean is vast and old, so even a small concentration delivered for millions of years adds up to a large total mass.

The Ocean's Salt Budget

A budget tracks inputs and outputs. Inputs include river discharge, hydrothermal exchange, atmospheric deposition, and groundwater. Outputs include the formation of evaporite deposits, incorporation into sediments and pore water, sea spray, and ion exchange with oceanic crust. The ocean is not a sealed container.

The residence time of an ion is the amount in the ocean divided by its input or removal rate. Sodium has a residence time of tens of millions of years, so it mixes throughout the ocean many times before being removed. That long memory is why seawater composition is so uniform and so slow to change.

Over millions of years, the budget has kept salinity within a range that supports life. That does not mean the ocean has always had exactly today's salinity. During periods when large evaporite basins formed, or when ocean circulation changed, salinity shifted. The point is that removal processes respond to concentration and keep the system from running away.

A useful analogy is a bathtub with a partly open drain. The tap runs slowly, the drain removes water at a rate that depends on the water level, and the level settles near a steady value. The ocean's salt budget works in a similar feedback sense, though the real chemistry is far more complex than a single drain.

Why Some Seas Are Saltier

Salinity varies because evaporation and precipitation differ by region. In the subtropics, evaporation exceeds rainfall, so surface water becomes saltier and denser. Near the equator and in high latitudes, rainfall and river input can dilute the surface layer. The Red Sea and the Mediterranean are saltier than the open ocean because they lose water to evaporation and exchange only through narrow connections.

Sea ice changes salinity in a different way. When seawater freezes, the ice crystal lattice mostly excludes salt, leaving the remaining liquid brine saltier. The dense brine sinks and can contribute to deep-water formation. Melting sea ice does the reverse, releasing relatively fresh water at the surface.

Estuaries show the transition between river and ocean. Fresh water flows out over denser salt water, and mixing depends on tides, wind, and the shape of the coast. The result is a gradient rather than a sharp boundary, and the gradient supports distinct ecosystems.

Depth also matters. Below the surface mixed layer, salinity often changes rapidly through a halocline. Because salt water is denser than fresh water, salinity together with temperature controls the vertical stability of the ocean. That stability shapes how heat, carbon, and nutrients move between the surface and the deep sea.

What Salinity Does to Water and Life

Salinity affects density, freezing point, and the behavior of water molecules. Dissolved ions interfere with the hydrogen-bond network that gives water its unusual properties. Salt water freezes at a lower temperature than fresh water and is denser at a given temperature, which is why salty water can sink beneath fresher water.

Organisms must manage the osmotic pressure created by dissolved ions. A cell in seawater tends to lose water to its surroundings unless it actively regulates its internal chemistry. Marine fish, algae, and bacteria have evolved different strategies, from pumping ions out to accumulating compatible solutes that balance the external concentration.

Salinity also shapes global circulation. Evaporation leaves surface water saltier and denser, and cooling makes it denser still. In a few high-latitude regions, that dense water sinks and begins a journey through the deep ocean. The resulting overturning circulation transports heat, carbon, and nutrients around the planet.

So the saltiness of the sea is a record of the rock cycle, the water cycle, and the ocean's internal chemistry. Rivers deliver ions slowly, hydrothermal systems exchange them, sediments bury them, and circulation mixes them. The ocean tastes salty because those processes have been running for a very long time, not because it has been quietly accumulating salt without limit.

Common misconceptions

The ocean is salty because rivers dump salt into it and nothing removes the salt.

Rivers do deliver dissolved ions, but the ocean also removes ions through evaporite formation, incorporation into sediments and pore water, sea spray, and exchange with oceanic crust. Over geological time, removal processes roughly balance input, so salinity does not rise without limit.

The ocean is equally salty everywhere.

Average open-ocean salinity is about 35 grams per kilogram, but it varies with evaporation, precipitation, river input, sea-ice formation, and depth. The Red Sea and the Mediterranean are saltier than the open ocean, while large river mouths and rainy equatorial regions can be fresher.

Sea ice is frozen salt water, so melting it adds salt to the ocean.

Sea ice mostly excludes salt as it forms, so the ice is relatively fresh and the remaining liquid becomes saltier. Melting sea ice therefore releases relatively fresh water, while brine rejection during freezing can make the underlying water saltier and denser.

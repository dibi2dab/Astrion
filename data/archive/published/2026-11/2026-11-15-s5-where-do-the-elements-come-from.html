Space & Astronomy

Where do the elements come from? The oxygen in your lungs, the calcium in your bones, and the iron in your blood were not present at the beginning of the universe. The early universe was almost entirely hydrogen and helium. Everything heavier was built later, mostly inside stars and in the violent events that end some stellar lives. That means the periodic table is also a history book. Each element records a different process, a different temperature, and often a different kind of stellar death.

8 min read

Key takeaways

  • The Big Bang produced mostly hydrogen and helium, with only traces of lithium and deuterium.
  • Stars fuse hydrogen into helium, then helium into carbon and oxygen, and massive stars continue up to iron.
  • Fusion beyond iron absorbs energy, so the heaviest elements require neutron capture in explosive environments.
  • Supernovae and neutron-star mergers disperse newly made elements into the gas that forms later stars and planets.
  • The elements in living things come from several different processes, not a single cosmic source.

The Inventory: A Universe Mostly Hydrogen

By mass, the universe is about three-quarters hydrogen and one-quarter helium. The remaining elements make up only a small fraction, yet that fraction includes everything needed for planets, oceans, and life. The abundance pattern is not random. Light elements are common, iron is relatively abundant, and elements such as gold and uranium are rare. Any successful account of the elements has to explain both the overall inventory and the details.

The inventory is measured in several ways. Astronomers read the spectra of stars and interstellar gas. They analyze meteorites, which preserve ancient material from the solar system's formation. They also observe the cosmic microwave background, which records the composition of the universe at about 380,000 years after the Big Bang. All of these lines of evidence point to the same broad history.

The First Elements: Big Bang Nucleosynthesis

In the first minutes after the Big Bang, the universe was hot enough for protons and neutrons to fuse into light nuclei. This period is called Big Bang nucleosynthesis. It lasted only about 20 minutes. As the universe expanded and cooled, the temperature dropped below the threshold needed for further fusion, and the process stopped.

The main product was helium-4, along with deuterium, helium-3, and a small amount of lithium-7. The reaction network could not proceed efficiently to carbon because there is no stable nucleus with mass number 5 or 8. A proton or neutron added to helium-4 produces lithium-5 or helium-8, both of which are unstable. That gap blocked the path to heavier elements in the early universe.

The first elements were light

Big Bang nucleosynthesis produced hydrogen, helium, and traces of lithium. It did not produce carbon, oxygen, or iron. Those elements required stars.

The predicted abundances match observations remarkably well. The observed primordial helium fraction, the deuterium abundance in distant gas clouds, and the lithium abundance in old stars are broadly consistent with a hot, dense early universe. The lithium-7 prediction is the least secure part of the comparison, which is one reason astronomers continue to study old stars and the early universe.

Stars as Element Factories

A star is a gravitationally bound ball of plasma that shines because fusion releases energy in its core. In stars like the Sun, the dominant process is the proton-proton chain, which combines hydrogen nuclei into helium. More massive stars use the CNO cycle, in which carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen act as catalysts for hydrogen fusion. Both processes turn hydrogen into helium and release energy.

When a star exhausts the hydrogen in its core, the core contracts and heats up. Hydrogen fusion continues in a shell around the core, and the outer layers expand. In a star like the Sun, the core eventually becomes hot enough for helium fusion. The triple-alpha process combines three helium-4 nuclei into carbon-12. Further alpha captures can produce oxygen-16, neon-20, and magnesium-24.

These reactions explain why carbon and oxygen are common in the universe. They are built in the late stages of ordinary stars and then ejected into space. The ejected material mixes with interstellar gas, and later generations of stars and planets form from that enriched material. The Sun is a later-generation star, so it started with a small but crucial supply of heavy elements.

The Iron Limit and Stellar Death

Stars more massive than about eight times the Sun can continue the sequence. After helium fusion, the core contracts and heats until carbon fusion begins. Then neon, oxygen, and silicon fusion follow. The star develops a layered structure like an onion, with the heaviest elements in the center and lighter fusion shells farther out.

The sequence stops at iron. Iron-56 has one of the highest binding energies per nucleon of any nucleus, which means it is near the bottom of the nuclear energy valley. Fusion reactions that build nuclei lighter than iron release energy. Fusion reactions that build nuclei heavier than iron absorb energy. A star cannot sustain itself by fusing iron; doing so would drain energy rather than supply it.

Once a massive star builds an iron core, it has only minutes to live. The core can no longer generate pressure through fusion, so it collapses under gravity. The collapse produces a core-collapse supernova, an explosion that blows off the outer layers and leaves behind a neutron star or a black hole. The explosion also provides the energy and neutron flux needed to make some of the heaviest elements.

The supernova does more than destroy the star. It disperses the elements that were made in the core and in the explosion itself. Those elements become part of the interstellar medium. The next generation of stars inherits them, and planets can form from the enriched gas. The calcium in your bones and the iron in your blood were released by stars that died before the Sun formed.

Forging the Heaviest Elements

Elements heavier than iron cannot be made by ordinary fusion. They require neutron capture, a process in which a nucleus absorbs neutrons and becomes a heavier isotope. If the nucleus has too many neutrons, it can undergo beta decay, converting a neutron into a proton and moving the element up the periodic table. Two versions of this process matter.

  • The s-process is slow: a nucleus captures neutrons over thousands of years, and beta decay keeps pace. It operates in asymptotic giant branch stars and produces elements such as strontium, barium, and lead.
  • The r-process is rapid: a nucleus captures many neutrons in a fraction of a second, producing highly neutron-rich isotopes that later decay. It requires extreme neutron densities, such as those in neutron-star mergers or core-collapse supernovae.
  • The p-process and related proton-rich processes make some rare isotopes through photodisintegration and charged-particle reactions.

The r-process is responsible for many of the heaviest elements, including gold, platinum, and uranium. The exact sites are still debated, but neutron-star mergers are a confirmed source. In 2017, the gravitational-wave event GW170817 marked the merger of two neutron stars. Follow-up observations found signatures of heavy elements in the ejected material, linking the merger to r-process nucleosynthesis.

The 1957 review by Margaret Burbidge, Geoffrey Burbidge, William Fowler, and Fred Hoyle, often called B2FH, laid out the framework for stellar nucleosynthesis. It connected the observed abundances to specific nuclear processes in stars. Later work refined the picture, especially for the heaviest elements, but the core idea remains: the periodic table is built by stars and by their deaths.

Reading Your Own Atoms

The elements in a human body have a mixed history. Hydrogen came from the Big Bang. Carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen were made in stars and dispersed by stellar winds and supernovae. Phosphorus and sulfur have stellar origins as well. Iron came from supernovae and from the gradual enrichment of the galaxy. Heavier trace elements, such as iodine and zinc, involve neutron-capture processes.

This is why the phrase we are made of starstuff is useful but incomplete. The hydrogen in water is older than the stars. The carbon and oxygen were forged in stars. The iron was assembled near the end of a massive star's life. A single human body contains atoms from several different cosmic factories.

The story is not finished. Stars are still making helium, carbon, and oxygen today. Massive stars are still exploding, and neutron stars are still merging. The interstellar medium is gradually becoming richer in heavy elements, and future generations of stars will form from that enriched material. The periodic table is still being written.

The next time you look at a chart of the elements, it may help to read it as a timeline. The lightest entries are relics of the first minutes. The middle entries are products of stellar fusion. The heaviest entries are souvenirs of catastrophic stellar events. The elements are not just a list of substances. They are a record of how the universe assembled its complexity.

Common misconceptions

All of the elements were made in the Big Bang.

The Big Bang made mostly hydrogen and helium, with small amounts of deuterium, helium-3, and lithium-7. Carbon and heavier elements were made later inside stars and in stellar explosions. The early universe did not have the time or the conditions to build them.

Stars burn elements like a fire burns wood.

Stars do not undergo chemical combustion. They fuse atomic nuclei in their cores, converting a small amount of mass into energy. The word burning is a shorthand for nuclear fusion, not a chemical reaction with oxygen.

All elements heavier than iron come from supernovae.

Supernovae contribute to the production of heavy elements, but neutron-star mergers are also a major site of the rapid neutron-capture process. The slow neutron-capture process in asymptotic giant branch stars makes another set of heavy elements. The origin of the heaviest nuclei is a combination of several environments.

Iron is the heaviest element that stars can make.

Iron is the heaviest element that can be made by ordinary fusion while releasing energy. Elements heavier than iron are made mainly by neutron capture, not by the normal fusion sequence in a stellar core.

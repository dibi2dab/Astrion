Why do bridges have expansion joints? A bridge looks rigid, but its length changes with the weather. On a hot afternoon a long steel span can be centimeters longer than it was on a cold night, and a concrete deck expands as the sun heats it. Without a place for that movement to go, the structure would push against its supports and crack.

5 min read

Key takeaways

  • A material expands when heated and contracts when cooled; the change is proportional to length and temperature change.
  • A 200-meter steel bridge can change length by several centimeters over a typical annual temperature range.
  • Expansion joints create a controlled gap, and bearings allow the deck to slide or rotate while carrying load.
  • Restrained thermal movement produces large forces, which is why the movement is designed for rather than blocked.

A Bridge Is Never the Same Length Twice

Most materials expand when heated. The atoms vibrate with greater amplitude and settle into a slightly larger average spacing. The effect is small for a short object and significant for a long one. A meter of steel grows by about 12 micrometers per degree Celsius, which sounds negligible until it is multiplied by a long span.

Plug in a 200-meter steel bridge and a 40 degree Celsius swing between a cold night and a hot day. With a coefficient near 12 times 10 to the minus 6 per degree Celsius, the change is about 9.6 centimeters. That is not a rounding error. It is a movement that must be accommodated somewhere.

Joints, Bearings, and Fixed Supports

An expansion joint is a gap or a sliding assembly that lets two parts of the structure move relative to each other. On a road bridge, the joint must also carry traffic smoothly, keep water out, and resist debris. The visible metal comb or rubber strip at the deck end is only the surface expression of a designed movement path.

Bearings are the components that transfer load from the deck to the piers while allowing movement. A fixed bearing resists translation and rotation, while an expansion bearing permits sliding or rotation. A typical bridge combines fixed and expansion bearings so that the deck can move in a controlled direction without drifting sideways.

The design question is not whether the bridge moves. It is where the movement is allowed and how the forces are routed. A bridge with no expansion provision would push against its abutments, bend its piers, or crack its deck. The resulting forces can be far larger than the loads the structure was designed to carry.

Movement must go somewhere

Restraining thermal expansion does not make it disappear. It converts the movement into stress, and the structure must resist that stress somewhere else.

What Happens When Joints Fail

Expansion joints are exposed to traffic, salt, water, and debris, so they are among the most maintenance-intensive parts of a bridge. A failed joint can let water reach the bearings and the deck edges, accelerating corrosion. Debris can jam the gap and prevent movement, which transfers force into parts of the structure that were not designed for it.

A jammed joint can also create a rough ride and concentrated impact loads. The repeated pounding damages the concrete around the joint, and the damage spreads. Maintenance crews inspect joints regularly because a small failure can grow into a structural problem.

Modern designs use modular joints, finger joints, and sliding plates to accommodate larger movements while keeping the road surface continuous. The choice depends on the expected movement, traffic volume, water exposure, and maintenance budget. No single joint type is best for every bridge.

Movement Beyond Temperature

Temperature is the most familiar cause of movement, but it is not the only one. Concrete shrinks as it cures, and long-term creep changes the shape of the structure under sustained load. Traffic adds dynamic deflection, and wind can cause vibration. Earthquakes introduce sudden, large displacements.

Bridges also move because of moisture and soil settlement. A pier can settle slightly, changing the alignment of the deck. Bearings and joints must tolerate small misalignments without binding. Designers combine the expected movements from all sources rather than treating temperature in isolation.

Seismic design adds a different requirement. A bridge may need to absorb or dissipate energy during an earthquake, and some bearings are designed to act as fuses or dampers. The goal is to protect the structure by controlling where movement and damage occur.

Why the Gap Looks Simple but Is Not

The visible gap is a compromise between structural needs and the driver's experience. A large gap accommodates movement but can feel like a bump. A small gap rides smoothly but may not provide enough travel. Engineers balance the two by estimating the range of movement and choosing a joint with margin.

The calculation uses the full temperature range the bridge will see, not just the weather on opening day. It also accounts for the temperature of the material, which can differ from air temperature. Dark asphalt in summer sun can become much hotter than the surrounding air, and steel can cool rapidly on a clear winter night.

Construction sequencing matters too. A bridge assembled at one temperature has a built-in length. The joint must be set so that the structure can move in both directions from that starting point, rather than using up all its travel in one direction at the first heat wave.

The next time a car thumps over a bridge joint, the sound is a sign of a structure making room for the seasons. The gap is not a defect. It is a deliberate allowance for a material property that cannot be argued away.

Common misconceptions

Concrete does not expand with heat, so only steel bridges need joints.

Concrete also expands when heated, though its coefficient is somewhat lower than steel's. Joints and bearings are used in concrete bridges as well, and the required movement depends on the span and the temperature range rather than on the material alone.

Expansion joints are only needed on very long bridges.

Any structure with a significant length or temperature exposure needs a plan for movement. Even moderate spans can develop enough thermal movement to crack if the ends are rigidly restrained. The amount of movement scales with length and temperature change.

A bridge with a visible gap is structurally weak.

The gap is a designed feature that protects the structure from restrained thermal movement. A bridge without a movement path can develop much larger forces and more serious cracking. The joint must be maintained, but its presence is not a sign of weakness.
